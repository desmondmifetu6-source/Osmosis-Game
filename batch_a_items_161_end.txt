170. [archimedes' principle]
A law of physics named after its discoverer, Archimedes of Syracuse that concerns the upward force or upthrust on any object fully or partly submerged in a fluid. It states that the buoyant force on an object is equal to the weight of the fluid it displaces. Mathematically, (buoyant force) Fb = m f g ... ., where m f is the mass of the fluid displaced by the body. Archimedes' principle can be expressed in other words that an object in a fluid such as water experiences an upthrust from the fluid which is equal to the weight of the fluid that the body displaces. The upward force is called the upthrust or buoyant force, expressed mathematically as: Upthrust = weight in air - weight in liquid. The resultant force on the A body is the difference between the upthrust and the body's weight. This difference is zero for a floating body, ie. upthrust and weight are equal and opposite. Hot air displaces a balloon, producing upthrust. This upthrust is balanced by the weight of the balloon. A hot air balloon rises when the upthrust on it is greater than its weight. A diver may attach lead weight to his suit if he wants to reach a greater depth. His weight will then be greater than the upthrust on him. For a given body to float in a given fluid depends on its weight and volume or the relative density (weight per unit volume). This is compared to that of the fluid in order to determine its buoyant force. If the body is less dense than the fluid, it will float. If the body is denser than the fluid, it will sink. The principle applies to both floating and submerged bodies and to all fluids, i.e., liquids and gases. Archimedes' principle is an important and underlying concept in the field of fluid mechanics.

217. [azimuth]
1. In astronomy, it is the arc of the horizon or the angular distance measured clockwise between a celestial object and the northern point of the horizon as seen by the observer. In other words, it is measured from the north point of the horizon to the intersection with the horizon of the vertical circle passing through a celestial body. In navigation, it is the angular distance usually measured clockwise from the north point of the horizon to the intersection with the horizon of the vertical circle passing through a celestial body. 2. In polar coordinates, it is also known as amplitude , argument or anomaly , which is the angle between the positive real axis and the vector representing a given complex number in the Argand diagram. 218. [azimuthal quantum number]
One of the four quantum numbers of an atomic orbital that determines its orbital angular momentum and describes the shape of the orbital. In other words, it is one of the four quantum numbers that determines the magnitude of the angular momentum of the electron around the nucleus. It also describes the shape of the orbital. It is denoted as l and takes the values 0, 1, 2, ..., n^-1, where n is the principal quantum number. Azimuthal quantum numbers 0, 1, 2 and 3, correspond to the s , p , d and f orbitals, respectively.

219. [azo compound]
An organic compound containing at least one of two doubly bonded nitrogen atoms with each atom linking two other groups through single bonds: R-N=N-R', in which R and R' can be either aryl or alkyl, for example, PhN=NPh, azobenzene. The more stable derivatives contain two aryl groups. The N=N group is called an azo group . An example of an azo compound is azo dye, which is a synthetic dye containing the azo group of two nitrogen atoms (-N=N) connecting aromatic ring compounds. Azo dyes are usually red, brown or yellow and make up about half the dyes produced. They are manufactured from aromatic amines.
