4. [cancellation]
The method of simplifying an expression by eliminating terms, for example, by dividing the numerator and the denominator by a common factor. For example, dividing both the numerator and the denominator of 9 / 24 by 3 gives 3 / 8 . Identical terms can be simplified by removing from both sides of an equation by subtraction or division, for example, 3 x + y = y + 6 can be simplified to 3 x = 6 and again to x = 2, by cancellation of a common factor on both sides.

5. [cancellation laws]
The cancellation law of addition states that for any real numbers a , b , c , if a + b = a + c , then b = c . In other words, any real number can be added on both sides of an equation and the equality will still hold. The cancellation law of multiplication says that both sides of an equation can be multiplied by any real number and the equation will still hold. For example, in a binary operation * on the set Z , the cancellation law states that (i) if p , q and r are in z and p * q = p * r , then q = r ; (ii) If q * p = r * p , then q = r . The law holds for addition, subtraction, multiplication and division of real and complex numbers, except for multiplication by zero and division of zero by another number. The law is not valid for some algebraic structures.

13. [capacitance]
An electrical property of a capacitor that determines how much charge can be stored in it for a given potential difference between its terminals. In other words, capacitance is the amount of electrical charge that a capacitor can store for each volt of applied potential or the ratio of the magnitude of charge, Q, on either plate to its electric potential V , between the plate. It is expressed as Capacitance ( C) = charge stored/potential difference: = Q V and measured in farads (the S.I unit of capacitance), with the symbol F . The farad is a very large unit and capacitors are more often rated in terms of microfarads (1 μ F = 10 -6 Farad). A capacitor in an ac circuit limits the current flow, similar to a resistor. This applies to an isolated conductor and does not apply to a charged insulator because an insulator cannot have a potential since charge cannot flow across the insulator to equalise the potential.

14. [capacitive reactance]
The opposition of capacitance to alternating current, caused by the inductance and capacitance in a circuit rather than by resistance. It is equal to the reciprocal of the product of the angular frequency of the current times the capacitance. C Its symbol is X C . The equation for calculating the amount of capacitive reactance in an ac circuit is given by: X c = 2 π fc , where X C is the capacitive reactance in ohms, f is the frequency in hertz and C is the capacitance in farads. A capacitor in an ac circuit limits the current flow similar to a resistor, therefore, applying Ohm's Law directly to a capacitor, gives V C = ICxC , where V C is the voltage across the capacitor, I C the current through the capacitor and X C the capacitive reactance. When capacitors are connected in parallel, the total charge, Q t that is stored for the same potential difference, V , equals the sum of the charges stored on the individual capacitors in the circuit: Q t = q 1 + q 2 + ... + q n . For capacitors connected in series, the total charge 1 Q t = 1 q 1 + 1 q 2 + ... + 1 q n . The total capacitance, 1 Q t , of capacitors in series is always less than the least capacitance in the circuit.

15. [capacitor]
A device for storing electric charge or energy for releasing at a specific time or rate. A capacitor consists of two plates the dielectric. Materials such as air, paper, mica and oil can be used as dielectrics. Most consist of two sheets of metal foil separated by a dielectric or insulator such as glass, paraffin, mica, oil, paper, tantalum or air and rolled into a tube. Capacitors are classified as non-polarised for use in alternating current (AC) circuits and polarised for use in direct current (DC) circuits. The capacitance of a capacitor depends primarily upon its shape and size and upon the relative permittivity ε r of the dielectric between the plates. Capacitance can be increased by increasing the area of the plates, by decreasing their separation or by changing the dielectric. If an electrical potential (voltage) is applied to the plates of a capacitor, for example, by connecting one plate to the positive and the other to the negative terminal of a direct current source, the plates will become charged, one positively and one negatively. If the externally applied voltage is then removed, the plates of the capacitor remain charged. When power is required, the circuit is switched on to conduct current between the plates and the charge is released. Capacitors are used in electronic circuits for blocking direct current while allowing alternating current to pass; in filter networks, for smoothing the output of power supplies; in the resonant circuits that tune radios to particular frequencies; in electric power transmission systems for stabilising voltage and power flow and for many other purposes.

21. [caratheodory's theorem]
The theorem that in Euclidean space every point in the convex hull of a given set S lies in some simplex with vertices in S. In other words, the theorem states that if a point x of R d lies in the convex hull of a set P, there is a subset P′ of P consisting of d + 1 or fewer points such that x lies in the convex hull of P′. Equivalently, x lies in an r -simplex with vertices in P, where r ≤ d . For example, consider a set P = {(0,0), (0,1), (1,0), (1,1)} which is a subset of R2 . The convex hull of this set is a square. Consider now a point x = (1/4, 1/4), which is in the convex hull of P. We can then construct a set {(0,0),(0,1),(1,0)} = P′, the convex hull of which is a triangle and encloses x and thus the theorem works for this instance, since |P′| = 3.

25. [carbon dating]
A method of calculating the age of once living or prehistoric objects or materials by measuring the decay of radioactive isotope of carbon ( 14 C) in them, developed by U.S. physicist Willard Libby in 1947. When an organism dies it stops taking in carbon, so the amount of carbon^-14 in its tissues steadily decreases. Because carbon^-14 decays at a constant rate, the time since an organism died can be estimated by measuring the amount of radiocarbon in its remains. The method is a useful technique for dating fossils and archaeological specimens from 500 to 50,000 years old.

27. [carbon-nitrogen ratio]
The ratio of the mass of organic carbon to the mass of total nitrogen in a particular organic substance, used as an indicator for nitrogen limitation of plants and other organisms. For example, a C:N ratio of 24:1, means that there are 24 units of carbon to 1 unit of nitrogen which is the proper carbon and nitrogen that a microorganism must have in order to sustain its C health. Also, for maintaining a compost pile in a good condition, the composting microorganisms, require the correct proportion of carbon for energy and nitrogen for protein production with C:N ratio of around 25-30:1. If the C:N ratio is too high (excess carbon), decomposition slows down. If the C:N ratio is too low (excess nitrogen) the compost pile becomes stinky. It is important that in selecting crops and cover crops rotation, C:N ratio of certain plants that may be included in the rotation should be considered. The following are the C:N ratios of a few crops: rye straw^-82:1, wheat straw^-80:1, oat straw^-70:1, young alfalfa hay^-13:1, mature alfalfa hay^-25:1, and alfalfa^-11.3. The ratios change as the plant grows, so the C:N ratio in a young plant will be much less than that of a matured plant. C:N ratio can identify whether molecules found in the sediment under study come from land-based or algal plants. It has a direct impact on residue decomposition and also nitrogen cycling in soils and can be used in analysing sediments and compost. It also serves as a tool for understanding the sources of sedimentary organic matter, which can lead to information about the ecology, climate, and ocean circulation at different times in Earth's history.

42. [casting out nines]
A method of checking arithmetical calculations that relies on the fact that, modulo 9, the sum of the digits of a sum or product equals the sum or product of the sums of the digits of the numbers added or multiplied, where the process of adding digits is repeated where necessary until the result is less than nine. For example, it can be determined that 365 x 248 cannot equal 90520, since adding the digits of each of the multiplicands yields 14, whose digits sum to 5, so that their product should have the same nines-complement as 5 × 5 = 25, that is 7, whereas similarly casting out nines from the supposed product gives 8. However, since this is merely a necessary and not a sufficient condition, the process can only be used to detect errors, but can neither determine nor verify a result.
