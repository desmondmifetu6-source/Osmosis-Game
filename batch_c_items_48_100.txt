69. [centripetal acceleration]
The acceleration of a body which travels along a circular track at constant speed. The acceleration is directed radially toward the centre of the circle and has a magnitude equal to the square of the body's speed along the curve divided by the distance from the centre of the circle to the moving body. The force causing this acceleration is directed also toward the centre of the circle and is named centripetal force. An example of centripetal acceleration is the acceleration toward the centre that holds a satellite in an elliptical orbit. The magnitude of centripetal acceleration, a c = v 2 / r , where a c is centripetal acceleration, v is velocity in metres per second and r is the radius of the circle in metres. The higher the velocity, the greater the centripetal acceleration; and the smaller the curve of the path being travelled, the greater the centripetal acceleration.

70. [centripetal force]
The force which makes an object move on a circular path or acts radially inward on an object moving in a curved path or acts tangentially to the object's path. It is that part of total gravitational force that keeps a body in its circular track and it is always directed towards the centre of the circle. For example, with a ball whirled in a circle at the end of a length of string, the centripetal force is the tension in the string and the reaction to this force is the centrifugal force. If the string breaks, the ball will move offin a straight line. The straight line motion in the absence of the constraining force is an example of Newton's first law. For a body of mass, m , moving at constant speed, v , around a circle of radius, r , the magnitude of the centripetal force, F c is expressed as F c = mv 2 / r . The gravitational attraction of the Sun is acentripetal force acting on Earth. This force produces the centripetal acceleration of the orbital motion.

It occurs in nature in two forms, conventionally described as positive and negative in order to distinguish between the two kinds of interaction between particles. Generally charged substances have an imbalance of positive and negative charges and a net charge that exerts a force on other charged substances. This is a very large quantity and therefore, measured in micro-coulombs (1 μC =1 × 10 -6 C) and nanocoulombs (1 nC = 1 × 10 -9 C). The value of elementary charge is approximately 1.602 x 10 -19 coulombs (C), and the charge of the electron is approximately^-1.602 x 10 -19 C.
